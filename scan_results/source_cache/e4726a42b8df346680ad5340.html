Goodbye to the Grind

How Kaffi Valeria Serves History and Happiness by the Cup

Words by
Beth Rogers

Photography by
Art Bicnick

The oldest known evidence of coffee in Iceland is a letter that Lárus Gottrup, a lawyer in Þingeyri, wrote to Árni Magnússon, a professor and manuscript collector, on November 16, 1703. They had spoken at the Alþingi (national Parliament meeting) that summer, and Árni was upset that his friend had forgotten to send him the coffee he had requested by spring ship from Copenhagen. To avoid leaving Árni stimulant-free, Gottrup sent 114 g of coffee beans (about a quarter of a pound) and noted that he himself did not like coffee: “After all, I’m not a fan of it.” 

Nowadays, cafés dot the Icelandic landscape, from the bustling streets of Reykjavík to the most remote rural villages, each with its own character and charm, yet all sharing the same commitment to keep the community buzzing. And in one small West Iceland town, a fresh brew is bubbling: Kaffi Valeria, a specialty café steeping tradition and innovation in a country with a caffeine history as deep and intriguing as a cup of its finest roast. 

Homebrew

Like most modern nations, Icelanders rely on a nudge of warmth and caffeine to get themselves going in the morning. Icelanders consume an average of 8.3 kilogrammes (about 18.3 pounds) of coffee per person every year, with a long-standing coffee culture that sees them drinking it at all hours of the day, from the morning ritual to social gatherings. In Nobel laureate Halldór Laxness’ works, coffee comes up frequently as a sign of hospitality, togetherness, and comfort. In Independent People, he writes: “Presently the smell of coffee began to fill the room. This was morning’s hallowed moment. In such fragrance the perversity of the world is forgotten, and the soul is inspired with faith in the future…”

Colombian Jan van Haas and Icelander Marta Magnúsdóttir have woven a modern tale of resilience, innovation, and community through their journey bringing coffee beans from Colombia to Iceland and opening their café, Kaffi Valeria, in Grundarfjörður, West Iceland. Their story begins with Jan’s departure from Colombia, driven by difficult circumstances, and his arrival in Iceland, where he found not just a refuge but a home. Marta recalls Jan’s early days travelling the world, saying, “He had to leave Colombia and he ended up in Spain, and we met in Sardinia. A few years later, he was here.”

It is so fantastic that I get to be on the other end of the production process, showing people what good coffee means, respectfully handling the farmers’ product. - Marta Magnúsdóttir

In the village of less than 1,000 people, Kaffi Valeria stands as a beacon of Colombian coffee culture. Owned and operated by the husband-and-wife team, the café reflects Jan’s dedication to blending the rich coffee traditions of his homeland with the communal ethos of his adopted country. “As you walk into the shop, you notice the books of Gabriel Garcia Marquez, both in Spanish and Icelandic, then you hear Latin music and smell the fresh coffee aroma,” Jan shares, describing the ambiance that envelops visitors upon entering the café. 

On the outside, the tiny house that is home to the coffee shop is painted with bright red coffee cherries and illustrations of Jan and Marta. On the inside, it opens to a warm, terracotta-coloured storefront decorated with plants, hanging vines, and signs inviting customers to try ordering in Icelandic. The clay coffee cups are solid and heavy, painted turquoise around the rim. Like everything in the space, they were designed by Jan and Marta, and then shaped and fired by Colombian artists. “Everything in the shop is here because I like it,” Jan says. 

Two Coffea arabica plants live in pots within the shop, three determined seedlings sprouting at the bottom of one shrub. Their glossy, dark green leaves release a faint, herbaceous scent, mingling with the sharp, smoky aroma of roasted beans. As the seasons change, the flowers give way to coffee cherries, initially green but maturing into a vibrant red, each one cradling the precious coffee beans – the seeds of the plant – within. Next to the hulking, black roaster sits a burlap bag of green coffee beans, wafting their fresh, grassy scent, which mingles with an earthy, wine-like sweetness that signals the beginnings of fermentation.

Like all the things you’ll find inside Kaffi Valeria, Jan and Marta potted the Coffea plants because they like them – and to see if they would take. Coffee does not grow in Iceland: it grows best near the equator and at high altitudes, above 2,000 metres [6,500 feet]. With most cultivation occurring in the Americas, the Caribbean, Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, coffee grows about as far from Iceland as you can get. The three cherries Jan and Marta were able to coax from their tree last year are not even enough to produce one shot of espresso. 

As Marta puts it, “We like to have the trees here to show people where the drink they are drinking comes from, and also that many coffee cherries are needed for their espresso. Somewhere – in our case, in Colombia – there is a farmer who did all the work of planting, harvesting, processing, shipping, and more so that they can enjoy a simple cup of coffee.” As Jan puts it, “There is no such thing as ‘just a coffee!’”

Downstairs, the couple have worked hard to refurbish what was once a dreary, dirt-floored cellar into a colourful, inviting space of low ceilings, hanging plants, twinkling lights, and comfortable cushions. Those who live in Grundarfjörður are just as welcome as tourists who pass by, hoping to get photos of the imposing Kirkjufell mountain or stop at a restaurant up the street for a bite of fresh fish from the harbour.

A brief history of coffee in Iceland

Coffee was not always king in Iceland. Before Árni Magnússon whinged about the lack of java on the island in 1703, Icelanders, like citizens of many other countries, drank beer throughout the day, though it was usually watered down. English sailors in the 1700s drank watered-down rum, known as grog, and the origin of the word ‘groggy,’ which has now become a synonym for sleepy, slow, and drunk-seeming behaviour, whether you have been imbibing or not. By the time of the Industrial Revolution in the 1760s, caffeine had become a clear favourite among European workers, who needed a jolt of energy to stay focused throughout long hours at work. 

In Iceland, coffee was initially only available to the wealthy, who could afford the expensive import prices. As more Icelanders picked up the habit, coffee beans dropped in cost, appearing in price lists starting in 1776 at 32 shillings a pound, slightly lower than the common valuation of small coffee pots. By 1819, Icelanders were importing 44 tonnes of coffee per year, amounting to 800 grammes of precious beans for each Icelander. 

Writing in 1849 from the island of Flatey, West Iceland, Reverend Ólafur Sívertsen observed Icelanders’ love of the dark brew:

Now there is hardly a farm in the west that does not enjoy coffee, so to speak, on equal footing with food, in some places twice, if not three times a day, and strong to boot. Coffee drinking has become so popular that it’s considered impolite among passers-by and sailors to not sit down for a coffee, no matter the conditions at sea or the weather. The farmer can hardly go to of his duties until he has had this refreshment, and the labourer can hardly go to work until he has been gladdened by a cup of coffee.

Green beans

In the future, besides perfecting the shop, Jan would like to expand their coffee operation to build relationships with more independent farmers in Colombia. He would especially like to encourage them to abandon old practices like razing the forest to create more fields for crops; instead, he would like to encourage them to plant among the forest. Forest farming is a gentler and more traditional practice, resulting in a smaller, but higher-quality crop. High-value specialty crops like coffee plants are protected under the forest canopy, which can be adjusted to their optimal shade level. The method is less devastating to the land and helps conserve habitat for wildlife.

This commitment to small-batch, sustainable coffee practices guides Kaffi Valeria’s approach to coffee sourcing. Marta also emphasises the importance of direct trade, a principle that allows the couple to pay farmers fair prices while ensuring the quality of their coffee. She proudly states, “When we started, Jan contacted his brother and his sister-in-law, Hector and Sandy, and they helped us to source the beans directly from farmers in Kanpo, Colombia.”

Jan’s journey into the world of coffee roasting and Marta’s foray into the Icelandic coffee scene underscore their commitment to quality, sustainability, and education. Jan admits, “It’s been a ‘fake it till you make it’ journey, a constant learning and improving.” The sentiment is echoed by Marta, who enthusiastically embraces their mission to educate customers about the origins and nuances of coffee. 

Both Jan and Marta view Kaffi Valeria not just as a business but as a medium for cultural exchange and a platform for celebrating the richness of Colombian coffee. Before I left the shop, I sampled jam made from coffee cherries, breathed deeply of the woody scent of coffee leaves, which can be used to brew tea, and observed coffee beans darkening in 30-second increments of roasting. “At first, we thought it would be nice to have the roaster going all day long, for the customers,” says Marta, laughing, “But it roasts so quickly. We realised that if we got distracted for just a few seconds, we could ruin a whole batch!”

Coming together

Kaffi Valeria’s integration into the Grundarfjörður community has been met with warmth and support. Marta reflects on the communal atmosphere that Kaffi Valeria fosters, noting how “the setting somehow encourages and sparks conversation among the guests.” Jan continues, “I enjoy how easily people start chatting with each other – the space makes it comfortable to start conversations with strangers and have a good time while drinking a cup of coffee. What I enjoy the most is seeing the faces of the customers after they have the first sip of coffee or chocolate, and they say to each other how good the drink is.” This sense of home extends beyond their café, touching on the broader Icelandic tradition where coffee is a reason to pause and connect with others.

As Kaffi Valeria looks to the future, Jan and Marta remain grounded in their vision of growth, sustainability, and outreach to the public. They envision Kaffi Valeria as a place where education and enjoyment go hand in hand, where customers can learn about the journey of coffee from bean to cup. Marta’s personal journey from scepticism to passion underscores the transformative power of their venture, not just for them but for the thirsty folks they serve. She muses on her evolution, “I didn’t get really excited about coffee until my first trip to Colombia when I visited the farmers. But then I said, ‘Oh wow, it is so fantastic that I get to be on the other end of the production process, showing people what good coffee means, respectfully handling the farmers’ product.’”

"Everything in the shop is here because I like it." - Jan van Haas

Their efforts to inspire and enrich do not end with good coffee – they want a good life for themselves and their customers, too. As I settle into my cushioned seat and huddle around my steaming cup, Marta reveals that she has a degree in education, and would like to write books for children in the future. Her stories, aimed at Icelandic children, offer a glimpse into the wider world, echoing the educational ethos of Kaffi Valeria. Through coffee and storytelling, Jan and Marta aim to foster a deeper understanding and appreciation of diverse cultures and the natural environment.

Getting off the ground(s)

The challenges of starting and running a business were difficult. The café was so old and needed so much renovation that it was difficult to find contractors willing to work on it. Though the couple purchased the house in 2021, they did not open the café until the summer of 2023. Marta reminisces about the early days of their venture, the uncertainty of COVID-19, and the logistical hurdles of importing a roasting machine, which was “more expensive than the coffeehouse itself!” 

Then there is the coffee. To get to Kaffi Valeria, it goes through a long process. That begins with the selection of beans in Colombia, which are harvested and shipped to Iceland. These beans are then roasted in the café to varying degrees, depending on the desired flavour profile. The roasting process is crucial, as it develops the beans’ flavour and aroma. Marta and Jan were pleasantly surprised that it was easy to source the beans for the café. Colombian authorities and the Colombian Coffee Association take care to guard their reputation of being a country producing high-quality coffee beans. Before exporting the beans, Sandy, Hector, and the local farmers need to go through several steps to ensure they meet governmental quality standards. Luckily, Iceland and Colombia made a free-trade business agreement in 2014, making imports more accessible for Jan and Marta.

Once they have arrived and are roasted, the coffee beans are ground. The coarseness of the grind depends on the brewing method to be used and affects the final taste of the coffee. Finer grounds are typically used for espresso, while coarser grounds are better suited for methods like French press or cold brew. The next step is brewing, where hot water extracts the coffee’s flavours and aromas. In Árni Magnússon’s day, before the ease and convenience of today’s brewing methods, dark-roasted coffee beans arrived by ship and were sold to jittery buyers in cloth bags. The beans were ground up or crushed by hand and boiled in a pan over the fire at home, which would result in a watery, bitter, acidic coffee. 

Finally, the brewed coffee is ready to be served. Each step of the coffee-making process, from bean to cup, plays a significant role in determining the beverage’s final taste and quality, offering a rich and complex experience that has captivated people around the globe. 

Despite Kaffi Valeria’s commitment to serving the most delicious cup of coffee they can offer to every customer, Marta is no coffee snob. When I observe that this coffee is much better than the dehydrated coffee crystals with which I started my drive to Snæfellsnes that morning, she smiles. “Nothing wrong with instant coffee. The best cup of coffee is the one that you like,” she says, offering me a cinnamon roll. 

Bean bliss

For Jan and Marta, coffee serves as a comforting constant, a source of warmth and community, in a land that can often feel isolated from the European mainland, or even the sister countries of Northern Europe. Jan and Marta’s journey with Kaffi Valeria is more than a story of a coffee shop; it is a narrative of migration, adaptation, and the creation of a new home. It reflects the broader story of Iceland’s coffee culture – a story of how an island in the North Atlantic has embraced the world through its love of coffee, forging connections across continents and cultures. 

There is no such thing as “just a coffee!”

For the two owners, this is at the heart of what they do in their cheerful little coffee shop by the sea in Grundarfjörður. Marta doesn’t want to compare her little coffee shop to larger chains. She and Jan don’t want that kind of stress. Right now, they are the only employees of Kaffi Valeria and are happy not to be anyone’s boss. “The goal is not to take over the world,” she insists, topping a latte with the perfect dollop of foam. “It’s to celebrate Colombia and Colombian coffee, right here in Iceland. You want to visit Colombia now, right?” 

As I sink into an overstuffed chair, I sip my coffee and watch the video of Colombian sun and kilometres of green trees, highlighting the very plants my coffee came from. After an afternoon by the sea in Snæfellsnes, I find I do feel a longing to walk among the coffee cherries and sunny brambles of the Colombian forests… but first, I’ll need more coffee.
