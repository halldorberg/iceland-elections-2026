📈Iceland fares better outside the EU

Economic growth, employment, productivity and purchasing power are higher in Iceland than in most EU states; the EU is grappling with debt, an energy crisis and declining competitiveness.

The NO side says Iceland is better off outside the EU: growth, employment, productivity, and purchasing power are higher in Iceland than in most EU countries. At the same time, the EU is grappling with debt, an energy crisis, and declining competitiveness.

A report by Áfram Ísland compares living standards and claims that Iceland outperforms EU countries in growth, employment, productivity, purchasing power, and public finances. Lilja Dögg Alfreðsdóttir, deputy chair of Framsóknarflokkurinn, says eurozone growth is 1% compared with 2.5% in Iceland. Ólafur Ragnar Grímsson, former president, argues that Iceland, Norway, and Switzerland are growing faster than the EU countries and that Iceland recovered from the crash faster than they did.

In his book, presented by Heimssýn, Frosti Sigurjónsson discusses the democratic deficit, ageing populations, unfunded pension systems, debt, and high energy costs within the EU. Kristinn Sv. Helgason writes in Heimildin that talks are unwise at this point because of the EU’s weaknesses, which he links in part to the Draghi report and political instability, and that there is a risk that investment in fisheries and energy will freeze.

Counter-arguments — the YES side responds directly

The image of a stagnant EU is outdated

Thomas Möller says opponents rely on an outdated image of Europe: the EU economy grew by 14% in 2008–2023 and is the world’s second-largest economy, euro area growth (1.5%) compares well with the United States, and about 3.5 million companies were established in EU countries in 2023, most of them innovative firms.

The hidden cost of the króna is overlooked in the comparison

Óðinn Freyr Baldursson points out that Iceland’s success outside the EU carries hidden costs: around 1.000 billion krónur, about 20% of GDP, are tied up in foreign exchange reserves, and Icelandic households and businesses pay more than 100 billion a year in interest costs due to the instability of the króna — a cost that falls heaviest on those least able to bear it.

visir.is · Óðinn Freyr Baldursson →

The króna costs around 300 billion a year

Björgvin G. Sigurðsson estimates that an independent floating króna costs “a conservatively estimated 300 billion a year, divided equally among households, businesses and the public sector” and says the current currency system is “fully exhausted” — meaning that prosperity outside the EU comes at a higher price than the comparison figures cited by the NO side suggest.
