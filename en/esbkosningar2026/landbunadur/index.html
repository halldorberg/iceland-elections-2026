NO-side arguments · EU referendum · 29 August 2026

🚜Agriculture at risk

Tariff protection would disappear and domestic food production, especially dairy production, would not withstand competition; promises of lower prices for consumers are greatly exaggerated.

The NO side says that accession would remove tariff protection and that domestic food production, especially dairy production, would then not withstand competition. In its view, promises of lower prices for consumers are greatly exaggerated.

Herdís Magna Gunnarsdóttir, a dairy farmer, warns in Austurfrétt that dairy production would run into difficulties without tariff protection and doubts that food prices would fall significantly for consumers. Erna Bjarnadóttir writes in Eyjafréttir that claims of up to a 25% reduction in food prices do not hold up: the actual reduction is closer to 3%, and many goods are already duty-free. On BB.is she also points out that there are no examples of permanent exemptions from the EU’s Common Agricultural Policy.

Margrét Ágústa Sigurðardóttir, managing director of Bændasamtakanna, criticizes in Bændablaðið that the parliamentary resolution contains no negotiating objectives and that, without an analysis of interests, the nation cannot take an informed position. Ögmundur Jónasson, former minister, points out in Vikublaðið that the agriculture chapter was never opened in the 2009–2013 talks and says temporary exemptions are not enough.

Counter-arguments — the YES side responds directly

Negotiating objectives: food security, regional settlement and a ban on imports of live animals

Þórður Snær Júlíusson says that Iceland would fight for special arrangements in agriculture because of more difficult production conditions: “Production conditions in Iceland are more difficult than in most of Europe. That is why support must ensure food security, regional settlement and continued farming.” The ban on imports of live animals must also remain in place to protect Icelandic livestock breeds.

Sweden and Finland secured special arrangements in agriculture

Pawel Bartoszek points out that Sweden and Finland obtained a special arrangement in agriculture (northern aid) when they joined the EU, and that Malta received “rather extensive special arrangements in its accession treaty” that still apply — there are therefore precedents for special arrangements for agriculture in northern regions.

Opportunities for Icelandic products in larger markets

Magnús Árni Skjöld Magnússon says that Icelandic farmers could make use of their distinctive position and expand into larger markets with Icelandic products; he asks whether the state of agriculture is really so good “that it would not be worthwhile for farmers to find out whether there might be, even considerable, opportunities for strengthening it” in negotiations.

Food prices could fall by 25–30%

Thomas Möller argues, against claims that price reductions are exaggerated, that food prices could fall by 25–30% with membership and interest rates drop from about 9% to about 3%; a NO vote would close the door on these terms for generations.
