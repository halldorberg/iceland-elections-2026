💱The euro is not a magic solution

Growth in the euro area is far lower than in Iceland; Finland, with the euro, has the weakest growth in the Nordic region, and the cost of the euro is lower growth and higher unemployment.

The NO side claims that the euro is no magic solution: growth in the euro area is far lower than in Iceland. It points out that Finland, which uses the euro, has the weakest growth in the Nordic countries and that the trade-off of the euro is lower growth and higher unemployment.

Lilja Dögg Alfreðsdóttir, deputy chair of Framsóknarflokkurinn, says that growth in the euro area is 1% versus 2,5% in Iceland, and that the trade-off of the euro is lower growth and higher unemployment. Ólafur Ragnar Grímsson, former president, sets out economic arguments against membership on Heimssýn’s website and says that Iceland, Norway and Switzerland are growing faster than the EU states.

On Nútíminn, Ólafur Ragnar adds that no Nordic neighbouring country uses the euro except Finland and calls the EU debate ideological froth rather than facts. Einar Sigurðsson, chairman of the board of Ísfélagið, says in Eyjafréttir that Iceland is not ready for the euro and wants to postpone the vote while inflation is high.

Counter-arguments — the YES side responds directly

The króna has costs of its own

Óðinn Freyr Baldursson points out that maintaining the króna comes at enormous cost: about 1.000 billion krónur, around 20% of GDP, are tied up in foreign exchange reserves; households and businesses pay over 100 billion a year in interest costs because of the króna’s instability and less favourable borrowing terms; and currency exchange cost about 12,4 billion in 2025. The króna also amplifies economic shocks and deters foreign investment.

The króna costs around 300 billion a year

Björgvin G. Sigurðsson estimates the cost of an independent, floating króna at “a conservatively estimated 300 billion a year, divided equally between households, businesses and the public sector in the form of interest-rate differentials”, and says the current monetary arrangement has been “fully tested” — membership of the monetary union is the most important of the benefits of full membership.

dv.is · Björgvin G. Sigurðsson →

The growth comparison is misleading per person

Sveinn Agnarsson, professor of economics at HÍ, points out that although total economic growth in 2014–2025 was 2.8% in Iceland compared with 1.5% in the euro area, the picture reverses when looking at GDP per capita: 1.0% annual growth in Iceland versus 1.2% in the euro area — and fluctuations in growth are also much greater in Iceland (standard deviation 4.0 versus 2.8).

Vísindavefurinn · Sveinn Agnarsson →

Usurious interest rates have accompanied the króna for more than a century

Dóra Magnúsdóttir says the public and small businesses pay much higher interest rates than comparable nations, and that the interest burden has plagued Icelanders for more than a full century; the euro is the only realistic tool for escaping “interest-rate servitude” — large companies already settle in euros, while small family businesses and households are left behind with króna interest rates.
