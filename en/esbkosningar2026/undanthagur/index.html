🚫No permanent exemptions

No country has received a permanent exemption from the EU’s fisheries or agricultural policy; special arrangements are temporary and subject to the power of the Commission.

The NO side says that no state has received a permanent exemption from the EU’s fisheries or agricultural policy. Special arrangements are temporary and subject to the authority of the European Commission.

Hreinn Loftsson compares, on Heimssýn, the promises made during the 2009–2013 negotiations with the legal reality, and says that permanent exemptions in fisheries were never on offer. Erna Bjarnadóttir writes on BB.is that there are no examples of permanent exemptions from the EU’s agricultural policy; the Finns’ northern support was built into the regulatory framework rather than being an exemption. Ágúst Valves Jóhannesson of Til vinstri við ESB says that the EU grants only transition periods, but no permanent exemptions in fisheries, and refers to Malta’s experience.

Albert Jónsson, former ambassador, points to what he sees as an incompatibility between Iceland’s non-negotiable demands for control over natural resources and the EU’s fundamental rules on integration. Ögmundur Jónasson says in Vikublaðið that temporary exemptions are not enough, and Erna Bjarnadóttir adds on Heimssýn that special arrangements are not legally permanent.

Counter-arguments — the YES side responds directly

Sweden, Finland and Malta secured special arrangements

Pawel Bartoszek notes that Sweden and Finland secured special arrangements in their accession treaties and that Malta obtained “quite extensive special solutions … in the field of fisheries” — experience shows that special solutions for small states are a real part of accession treaties, not wishful thinking.

dv.is · Pawel Bartoszek →

All new member states received agreements tailored to their particular circumstances

Thomas Möller claims that the accession treaties of all new member states in recent decades were adapted to each country’s particular circumstances through special provisions, and that Iceland’s negotiating position has improved significantly — Iceland would bring the EU, among other things, about 700 thousand square kilometres of maritime area and a key strategic position in North Atlantic security matters.

Evrópuhreyfingin · Thomas Möller →

Special arrangements are the rule, not the exception

Thomas Möller argues that special arrangements are “the rule rather than the exception” within the EU, and that the Union has a strong history of tailoring accession treaties to the particular circumstances of island states; moreover, the allocation of fishing rights is based on historical use, which would safeguard Icelandic interests regardless of the wording on permanence.
