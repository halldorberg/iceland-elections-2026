YES-side arguments · EU referendum · 29 August 2026

💶Lower interest rates and a stable currency

The euro would lower interest rates for households and businesses (even from ~9% to ~3%), eliminate indexation and the króna risk premium, and create economic stability that the króna has never achieved.

According to the YES side, a stable currency is the main economic argument for membership. It argues that the euro would lower interest rates for households and businesses, potentially from around 9% to around 3%, eliminate indexation and the so-called króna premium. In its view, membership would thus create the economic stability that the króna has never achieved.

In an article on the Evrópuhreyfingin website, Dóra Magnúsdóttir compares Icelanders’ interest-rate problem to a flat tyre and says the euro is the only realistic tool for bringing rates down after a century of króna instability. Óðinn Freyr Baldursson writes on visir.is that the króna ties up 1.000 billion in foreign exchange reserves and costs over 100 billion annually in interest, while Björgvin G. Sigurðsson says in an article on dv.is that the króna costs about 300 billion annually. Thomas Möller claims that interest rates could fall from around 9% to around 3% and that the króna premium would disappear with membership.

Berglind Guðmundsdóttir, managing director of SJÁ, asks in an article on Vísir why Icelanders accept high interest rates and inflation that European households do not know. Magnús Árni Skjöld Magnússon, chair of Evrópuhreyfingin, believes indebted households are among the groups that would benefit most from membership because of lower interest rates and greater predictability. Óðinn Freyr Baldursson also argues that the business elite’s opposition to the EU is based on self-interest in an unstable króna economy, whereas lower interest rates and stability would benefit ordinary people.

Counter-arguments — the NO side responds directly

Nothing about interest rates is negotiated in an accession treaty

Haraldur Ólafsson, chairman of Heimssýn, points out that no provision on the level of interest rates would be negotiated in an EU accession treaty; interest rates are determined by each country’s economic conditions and risk. He says lower interest rates are a prerequisite for being able to adopt the euro, not an automatic consequence of membership.

Útvarp Saga · Haraldur Ólafsson →

The euro’s trade-off: lower growth, higher unemployment

Lilja Dögg Alfreðsdóttir, deputy chair of Framsóknarflokkurinn, says in an article on Heimssýn’s website that eurozone growth is about 1%, compared with 2.5% in Iceland. The trade-off for the euro’s low interest rates is lower growth and higher unemployment than Icelanders have.

Heimssýn · Lilja Dögg Alfreðsdóttir →

Finland with the euro — weakest economic growth in the Nordics

Ólafur Ragnar Grímsson, former president, points out that Finland is the only Nordic neighboring country that uses the euro and also has the weakest economic growth in the Nordics; Iceland recovered faster from the financial crash than the EU states. The real-world examples do not match the promises made about the euro.

Nútíminn · Ólafur Ragnar Grímsson →

Iceland is not ready for the euro

Einar Sigurðsson, chairman of the board of Ísfélagið, says in Eyjafréttir that Iceland is not ready for the euro while inflation is high; the Maastricht criteria would first have to be met, and the fisheries sector needs stability in the meantime.
