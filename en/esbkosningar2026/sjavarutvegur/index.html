🎣Control over fisheries to Brussels

The EU’s Common Fisheries Policy would hold undivided authority over fishery resources; the experience of Ireland and Malta shows that small nations come out second best.

The NO side says that the EU’s Common Fisheries Policy would hold exclusive power over the fishery resource if Iceland joined the union. It points to the experience of Ireland and Malta, which it says shows that small nations lose out in this system.

Hjörtur J. Guðmundsson writes in Skessuhorn that with membership, control over marine resources would shift to Brussels, where Iceland’s weight would be about 0.08%, and says Ireland’s experience is a warning. Hannes Hólmsteinn Gissurarson argues on the Heimssýn website that membership would cost control over fisheries and independent trade agreements. Hreinn Loftsson says in an article for Heimssýn that membership would mean Brussels controlling fisheries and energy policy.

Albert Jónsson, former ambassador, points to what he sees as an incompatibility between Iceland’s non-negotiable demands for control over resources and the EU’s fundamental rules on integration. Ögmundur Jónasson, former minister, demands clear answers in Vikublaðið on fisheries and agriculture and notes that the agriculture chapter was never opened in the 2009–2013 talks. Einar Sigurðsson, chair of Ísfélagið, says in Eyjafréttir that the fisheries sector needs stability and wants the vote postponed while inflation is high.

Counter-arguments — the YES side responds directly

Fishing track record decides — the quota would remain Icelandic

Thomas Möller, a board member of Evrópuhreyfingin, points out that fishing rights within the EU are based on historical use (relative stability); no other nations have a fishing track record for the main demersal fish stocks in Icelandic waters, and the quota would therefore continue to belong to Icelanders.

Evrópuhreyfingin · Thomas Möller →

Malta obtained special arrangements in fisheries

Pawel Bartoszek, chair of the Foreign Affairs Committee, points out that “Malta did in fact get fairly extensive special arrangements in its accession treaty … in the field of fisheries” and that fisheries and agriculture are matters for negotiation — the experience of small states shows that they can be negotiated.

dv.is · Pawel Bartoszek →

Full control over marine resources is a red line

Þórður Snær Júlíusson explains that Iceland’s negotiating objectives, according to the opinion of the majority of the Foreign Affairs Committee, stipulate “full control over marine resources, fisheries management, and the sustainable use of fish stocks” — control over the nation’s key resources is defined as a red line in the negotiations.
