YES-side arguments · EU referendum · 29 August 2026

🛒Lower prices and better living standards

Membership would lower food prices (figures from 3% up to 25–30% have been cited), increase competition in banking and insurance services, and bring consumers European consumer protection.

According to the YES side, membership would lower food prices, with figures ranging from 3% up to 25–30% having been cited. It also says membership would increase competition in banking and insurance services and bring consumers European consumer protection.

In articles on the Evrópuhreyfingin website, Thomas Möller claims that the best living standards in the world are found in EU countries and that food prices could fall by 25–30%. Magnús Árni Skjöld Magnússon says that businesses, indebted households, and rural areas would benefit most from membership, among other things because of a tariff-free market and predictability. Steinar Harðarson writes in Eyjafréttir that the EU is much more than an economic union; it also encompasses consumer protection, workers’ rights, and human rights.

On Reddit, the user Einn1Tveir2 has posted data showing that economic growth in Iceland (0.9%) is lower than in the EU (1.4%) and has been almost nonexistent per capita since 2018, presenting this as a direct response to the NO side’s economic arguments. Þorvaldur Ingi Jónsson writes on visir.is that natural resources alone do not explain high growth and that small nations such as Ireland and Malta benefit from the EU.

Counter-arguments — the NO side responds directly

The real reduction in food prices is close to 3%, not 25%

Economist Erna Bjarnadóttir rebuts claims of up to a 25% reduction in food prices with a concrete example: in a 2,590 krónur chicken wrap, the meat accounts for about 280–290 krónur, so a 25% reduction in meat prices would produce just under a 3% reduction in the price of the product. In addition, many products are already tariff-free from the EU under agreements from 2018, and products with active tariff protection account for only about 5.4% of household spending.

The price difference is explained by wages and transport costs, not the EU

Haraldur Ólafsson, chair of Heimssýn, says that when promises of cheaper food are examined more closely, the price difference turns out to be negligible — in the example of a chicken meal only about 65 krónur, not hundreds or thousands. He says the higher price level in Iceland is due to higher wages, transport costs, and the inefficiency of small scale, not to remaining outside the EU.

Dairy farmer: lower consumer prices unlikely, production at risk

Herdís Magna Gunnarsdóttir, a dairy farmer, says irresponsible claims are being made about agriculture and food prices: without tariff protection, domestic milk production would run into difficulties, while it is uncertain whether any reduction would be passed on to consumers to any significant extent.

Austurfrétt · Herdís Magna Gunnarsdóttir →

Living standards are already better in Iceland than in EU countries

A report by Áfram Ísland compares living standards: Iceland ranks ahead of EU countries in economic growth, employment, productivity, purchasing power, and the state of public finances. The organisation therefore says that joining the Union would bring no improvement in living standards.
