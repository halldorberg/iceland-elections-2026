YES-side arguments · EU referendum · 29 August 2026

🎓Opportunities for young people

Education, jobs and home purchases at affordable interest rates in 27 countries; high interest rates hit young people hardest.

The YES side says that membership would open opportunities for young people to study, work, and buy housing at affordable interest rates in 27 countries. It points out that high interest rates hit young people hardest.

Thomas Möller writes on the Evrópuhreyfingin website that the vote is above all about the future of young people: mortgage interest rates, study and work opportunities in 27 countries, and stability. In another article, he says that a yes would secure lower interest rates, stability, and better access to education and research, and reiterates that high interest rates hit young people hardest.

Counter-arguments — the NO side responds directly

The euro is not the solution for young people — an independent currency suits a volatile economy

Kjartan Magnússon, a board member of Ung gegn ESB, acknowledges that high interest rates have led many people, especially young people, to look to the euro as a possible solution, but says an independent currency is the better option because the Icelandic economy is based on volatile industries; Iceland recovered more quickly from economic crisis than the eurozone countries Greece and Spain.

Membership binds future generations to an almost irreversible commitment

Erlendur Magnússon says that the experience of the United Kingdom shows that leaving the EU is “almost impossible” and, for a small country like Iceland, “virtually impossible”; the current generation cannot allow itself “to bind our descendants in the shackles of the European Union for ever and ever” — the opportunity for young people lies in keeping the options open.

visir.is · Erlendur Magnússon →

Housing problems and youth unemployment are worse within the EU

An article on BB.is points out that young people’s housing difficulties are severe in many parts of the EU despite low interest rates: in many European countries, young people find it harder to buy housing, high rents create rental poverty traps, and youth unemployment within the EU has historically been much higher than in Iceland.
