🪑A seat at the table instead of EEA powerlessness

Iceland currently implements a large share of EU rules through the EEA without any voting rights. Full membership provides a seat in the Council of Ministers, the Commission and Parliament — small states carry relatively significant weight.

The YES side points out that Iceland already implements a large share of EU rules through the EEA Agreement without any voting rights. In its view, full membership would give Iceland a seat in the Council of Ministers, the Commission, and Parliament. It also argues that small states have proportionally significant influence within the Union.

Magnús Árni Skjöld Magnússon writes on the Evrópuhreyfingin website that Iceland adopts 75–80% of EU internal market rules without any influence and that the current EEA status is unacceptable for a sovereign state. Kristján Vigfússon strikes the same note, saying that EEA states have almost no formal influence on EU legislation; Iceland accepts rules without voting rights, which fits poorly with sovereignty. Hjálmar Vilhjálmsson, a board member of SJÁ, calculates using the Banzhaf method that each Icelander’s voting weight in the EU Council of Ministers would be about 37 times that of each German — today that weight is zero.

Páll Rafnar Þorsteinsson rejects concerns on the Evrópuhreyfingin website about the powerlessness of small states and says small nations have proportionally more representatives and the highest satisfaction with membership, with Ireland at 93%. In an article on dv.is, Björgvin G. Sigurðsson calls the EEA a temporary solution without democratic participation.

Counter-arguments — the NO side responds directly

0.08% weight — equivalent to a 5% share in one MP

Hjörtur J. Guðmundsson calls the seat at the table a myth: Iceland’s weight in the EU Council of Ministers would be about 0.08%, equivalent to a 5% share in one member of Alþingi, and in the European Parliament about 6 MEPs out of more than 700 — around 0.8%, corresponding to about half an MP. In the Commission, states in reality do not have representatives, since Commissioners are not permitted to favor their home country.

Ireland’s experience: “We never had a chance"

Hjörtur J. Guðmundsson points out that Ireland, with around five million inhabitants, was unable to prevent cuts to fishing quotas and the EU mackerel agreement despite having a seat at the table; leaders of Irish fishermen said they never had a chance. A seat at the table brings a small state painfully little real influence.

Skessuhorn · Hjörtur J. Guðmundsson →

6 seats out of 720 instead of full control

Hreinn Loftsson says in an article on Heimssýn’s website that instead of full control over fisheries and energy matters, Iceland would get only 6 seats out of 720 in the European Parliament; authority over the nation’s most important interests would shift to Brussels, where Iceland’s weight would be negligible.
